\section{Requirement Analysis}\label{sec:requirement-analysis}

The framework is an experimental deliberation layer, not a conventional application. A question enters, three heterogeneous agents debate it over three rounds, each agent's claims are checked against retrieved scientific evidence, and a trust-weighted answer leaves the system. The design must make that pipeline correct, reproducible, and auditable before it can support an efficacy claim.

The nonfunctional requirements follow from the research setting. Reproducibility is the first requirement: every result-affecting input, including the model checkpoint, prompt text, dataset revision, seed, option permutation, and code commit, must be recorded in a manifest. Auditability is the second: a reader must be able to trace a final answer back to the positions, claims, passages, and verdicts that produced it. Boundedness is the third: the trust vector must remain on the simplex, so no agent is silenced and no agent dominates. Portability is the fourth: the pipeline must run against local vLLM servers and against any OpenAI-compatible endpoint without changing the orchestration code.

Latency and cost are operational constraints. The debate is fixed at three rounds and three agents, and each model call is retried at most three times. That cap keeps a single question within a predictable budget and prevents a failed server from holding a run indefinitely. The final stack is designed for one 96\,GB card, with a pinned 69.73\,GB weight total and a 0.90 utilization sum; the development stack runs on one 48\,GB A6000. The system is inference-only, so no training cost or model-update risk is introduced.

Fault tolerance is local rather than global. A retrieval failure produces an unverifiable claim, a parser failure triggers the fallback extractor, and a model timeout produces an inconclusive position after the retry budget. None of these failures may silently fabricate a value. Security and privacy are handled by keeping API keys in the environment, never committing \texttt{.env}, and sharing gated dataset text only as identifiers and hashes.

\section{Detailed Methodology and Design}\label{sec:detailed-methodology}

\paragraph*{System overview.}
The architecture separates three concerns. The orchestration layer owns the round loop, the agent prompts, and the aggregation rule. The evidence layer owns claim extraction, source routing, retrieval, and verdicts. The evaluation layer owns the endpoints, the statistics, the records, and the manifest. The separation matters because the trust signal is only as good as the evidence layer, and the evidence layer must be auditable independently of the debate.

The design keeps the debate fixed at $N=3$ agents and $K=3$ rounds. Round~0 is record round~1; the two revision rounds are record rounds~2 and~3. Round~0 collects independent positions, and the revision rounds expose each agent to the other agents' positions and to its own current trust weight; an agent never sees another agent's weight. The evidence layer runs after each round's positions are fixed, so the trust update cannot change the positions that produced it. A closing pass recomputes trust from the final positions, and that vector is the one used by aggregation.

\paragraph*{Debate loop.}
The loop is a plain Python state machine. For each round it builds a prompt from the question, the rendered options, the agent's own previous position, its trust weight, and the peer positions truncated to 1{,}500 characters. The truncation keeps the three-agent transcript inside the 4{,}096-token development context. A model call is retried at most three times with exponential backoff; an empty completion or an unparseable position becomes an inconclusive position rather than a fabricated answer. The loop records every position, every prompt version, the retry count, and the elapsed time.

The three agents are deliberately heterogeneous. The development stack uses Qwen3.5-9B-AWQ, Gemma~4 12B, and Ministral-3-14B-AWQ; the final stack replaces them with Qwen3.6-27B-FP8, Gemma~4 26B-A4B-FP8, and Mistral Small 3.2 24B-AWQ. The different pre-training pipelines are intended to reduce correlated errors, but that assumption is tested only by the planned model-family and shared-error analyses, not by Gate~0.

\paragraph*{Options and answer parsing.}
The GPQA loader stores the correct answer first, and MMLU-Pro stores the dataset order. Neither order may reach a prompt, because a model can learn a position shortcut~\cite{zheng2024mcqbias}. The renderer shuffles the choices with a per-question seed derived from a SHA-256 hash of the question identifier and the run seed. The permutation is stored in the record, so the rendered order is reproducible across processes and resumes. Free-form items receive the literal instruction ``Answer format: free text'' and no fabricated options.

The parser prefers an explicit answer span: an \texttt{Answer:} or \texttt{Final answer:} line, a boxed expression, or the last line containing a letter or a number. It handles caret and LaTeX scientific notation, strips units before numeric comparison, and maps a letter or option text to the rendered option. Every record carries a parser version. Gate~0 showed why this is load-bearing: 17 of 90 generations opened with a formatting-only line and placed the answer on the next line.

\paragraph*{Claim decomposition.}
Agents are prompted to tag claims as \texttt{<claim id="cX">...</claim>} in the same generation. The parser extracts the tagged spans. If no tags are present, it falls back to sentence splitting, keeps items of at least three words, and caps the fallback at 20 claims. The trust configuration uses at most eight claims per agent. The fallback is necessary: in Gate~0, Qwen and Gemma tagged all 30 generations each, while Ministral left 5 of 30 untagged. Without the fallback, those positions would contribute no verifiable claims and would be treated as neutral by accident.

The claim text is what the retrieval and verifier layers see. Atomic claim decomposition follows FActScore, which checks each atomic fact against a knowledge source~\cite{min2023factscore}. Untagged prose is not verified. A judgment-style claim usually returns unverifiable and therefore does not move trust. The claim-level scope is a deliberate limit: the mechanism acts only on claims that the evidence layer can check.

\paragraph*{Claim-domain retrieval.}
Retrieval is routed by claim domain rather than by agent identity. Biomedical and clinical claims query PubMed and Semantic Scholar; physics, chemistry, mathematics, and computer-science claims query arXiv and Semantic Scholar; other claims query Semantic Scholar or OpenAlex. This replaces an earlier design in which agent~1 always used PubMed, agent~2 always used arXiv, and agent~3 always used Semantic Scholar. The earlier design confounded source, domain, and model family: an agent could appear more accurate because its assigned source covered the question's domain more easily.

Retrieved passages are deduplicated by DOI, arXiv identifier, or title. The retriever takes a small candidate set and selects the passages shown to the agent; the trust configuration uses three passages per claim. The live APIs are not exercised by the test suite. A retrieval failure is recorded, and the claim becomes unverifiable when no passage is returned. The retrieval layer is therefore designed and routed, but its live coverage and rate-limit behaviour remain operational risks.

\paragraph*{Verifier interface.}
The verifier is a separate interface with four implementations: lexical overlap, cross-encoder, natural-language inference, and an LLM judge. The default is lexical. A supported verdict requires support in the retrieved passage; a contradicted verdict requires an explicit contradiction; no evidence yields unverifiable. Evidence that supports and contradicts the same claim is recorded as unverifiable rather than counted in either direction. The interface records the verifier name and version with each verdict.

The lexical default is a diagnostic, not a validated scientific verifier. It scores claim-term coverage and can misread numeric or negated statements. The cross-encoder is optional and requires \texttt{sentence-transformers}; the LLM-judge hook exists but is not passed by the debate runner in the current branch. The human audit of 150--200 claim--passage pairs has not been completed, and the synthetic audit reports a balanced accuracy of 0.58. For that reason, the report makes no claim about verifier accuracy, and the planned matrix treats verifier quality as a moderator.

\paragraph*{Trust operator.}
Let $w_i^{(t)}$ be agent $i$'s weight at round $t$, $V_i$ the number of its claims that the verifier supports, $H_i$ the number it contradicts, and $n_i$ the number checked. The operator accumulates evidence in the log domain:
\begin{equation}
  \ell_i^{(t+1)} = \log w_i^{(t)} + \alpha \frac{V_i}{n_i} - \beta \frac{H_i}{n_i},
  \label{eq:trust-update}
\end{equation}
where $\alpha=0.5$ and $\beta=0.3$. One softmax turns the scores into weights, and a projection places the result on the polytope
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{W} = \left\{ w \in \mathbb{R}^{N} : w_i \ge f,\; \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i = 1,\; w_i \le \max(c, 1/N) \right\},
  \label{eq:trust-simplex}
\end{equation}
with floor $f=0.1$ and ceiling $c=0.9$. The projection guarantees the floor and the sum for every finite input. The ceiling is relaxed to $1/N$ only when $Nc<1$, a case with no feasible vector; $N=1$ returns $[1.0]$ because that is the only feasible vector. Neutral evidence, $V_i=H_i=0$, is an identity for an already feasible vector, so the weights do not drift toward uniform. Dividing by $n_i$ cancels verbosity, so duplicating every claim does not increase an agent's weight.

The implementation performs a closing pass on the final positions and exposes \texttt{trust\_for\_positions}. It also records \texttt{trust\_status} so an abstention is distinguishable from a zero weight. This timing matters: an earlier implementation updated trust before the final positions were fixed, which meant the aggregation used a vector that did not correspond to the answers being aggregated.

\paragraph*{Weighted aggregation.}
Aggregation is a weighted plurality, not a majority vote. Each agent's final parsed position is mapped to a canonical answer group. For each group $a$, the score is
\begin{equation}
  S(a) = \sum_{i:\,p_i=a} w_i,
  \label{eq:weighted-aggregation}
\end{equation}
and the group with the largest score wins. Ties resolve to the first observed answer. The majority answer is computed and stored alongside the weighted answer so the same transcript can be scored under both rules; it does not choose the treatment's final answer. This separation lets the pilot compare the weighted and unweighted winners without re-running the models.

\paragraph*{Injection protocol.}
The injection path is a one-shot stress test. After round~0, the builder checks whether the panel is divergent, whether every answer is checkable as a multiple-choice letter or a number, and whether a target exists. A unanimous panel is ineligible, as is a panel with fewer than two agents or without a strict 2/3 majority; ties and plurality-only panels are ineligible. The eligible plan fabricates a consensus and targets the agents that disagree with it by default; the \texttt{all} scope is also supported. The injected text is delivered as a one-shot user-turn addition in the second revision round (record round~2); the system prompt is unchanged, and round~3 carries no injection. Ineligible items are logged with a reason and are not fatal.

The injection is a controlled stress test, not natural sycophancy. It isolates the effect of social pressure from the effect of evidence. The manipulation check is a separate annotation task; it is not part of the confirmatory matrix.

\paragraph*{Runner, records, and manifest.}
The runner writes one JSON line per completed debate and appends it immediately, so a crash costs at most one question of GPU time. Re-running the same configuration skips completed question identifiers. Every record carries the question identifier, arm, seed, question, gold answer, raw and rendered options, permutation, rendered gold letter, raw answer, parsed answer, parser version, per-round answers, transcript, injection record, trust trajectory, final trust, verdicts, evidence, aggregation, and timing. The schema validator requires the final answer, its source, the parser version, and a valid trust simplex; it rejects a record rather than writing a plausible but unobserved value.

The manifest hashes the configuration files, the run configuration, the model checkpoints, the prompt file, the pinned dataset revisions, the code commit, the temperature, and the seed. The hash excludes the creation time, so re-running the same configuration later resumes instead of forking. The resume guard refuses to append when a result-affecting field differs, and it names the differing keys. The P0 gate exercises the guard with an identical manifest and a changed manifest; the changed manifest is refused.

\paragraph*{B7 external-answer ceiling.}
B7 is an optional appendix arm, not part of the Core or Full matrix. It sends each question to OpenAI GPT-6 Luna three times, records each sample, and reports the mean single-call accuracy with a question-level bootstrap confidence interval and the three-sample majority accuracy. Ties resolve to the first sample. The arm is a reference point for ``what does a strong single call get?'', not a competitor mechanism and not the internal B8-oracle arm. Its cost is projected at roughly \$3--9 per 1{,}000 questions at three samples.

\paragraph*{Experimental design.}
The study asks four questions. \textbf{RQ1:} Does evidence-grounded trust improve final-answer accuracy and correct-minority retention relative to a matched majority-vote control? \textbf{RQ2:} Does the effect reverse when verifier accuracy or evidence redundancy falls below a boundary? \textbf{RQ3:} How much of the effect depends on claim-level evidence rather than on any re-weighting? \textbf{RQ4:} How much of the remaining error is a shared-error floor that no independent verifier can remove?

The corresponding hypotheses are pre-registered. H1: B8 improves accuracy and CMR over B8-no-trust on pressure-eligible items. H2: the sign of the effect depends on verifier accuracy and redundancy, with a sign reversal at the predicted boundary. H3: the placebo arm, which shuffles verdicts, does not reproduce the B8 effect. H4: the oracle arm bounds the verifier bottleneck, and the shared-error floor limits the achievable selective risk. The Core matrix directly tests RQ1/H1; RQ2--RQ4 are exploratory, and the $q/\rho$ sweeps and shared-error analysis are N9 work outside the 24-run Core matrix. H3 and H4 depend on the WP-18 placebo/oracle extension.

A 300-item set with clean gold is better than a 600-item set with unreliable gold. The calibration batch is the first decision point: if the estimated annotation effort or defect rate is too high, the set is cut to 120 items rather than allowing the benchmark to delay the matrix.

\paragraph*{Endpoints and statistical analysis.}
Final-answer accuracy on pressure-eligible items is the primary endpoint. Accuracy is binary at the question level, scored against the gold answer, and paired across arms. Correct-minority retention (CMR) is the co-primary endpoint. On a pressure-eligible item, the round-0 panel has a strict wrong majority and at least one correct agent; CMR is the fraction of those items where an initially correct agent still holds the gold answer at the final round.

The secondary family contains appropriate revision rate, trust AUROC, trust Brier score, verifier balanced accuracy, and citation faithfulness. Citation faithfulness follows ALCE's evaluation of whether cited evidence supports generated claims~\cite{gao2023enabling}. CCR-0, CCR-E, and MPR-weighted are diagnostics. ECR is deprecated: it is a calibration proxy that is anti-correlated with correctness and is not a primary endpoint. The analysis reports counts and confidence intervals for every rate, not only a point estimate.

The unit of analysis is the question. Seed~1 is the pre-registered primary replicate for the exact McNemar test; seed~2 is the replication. Both seeds enter the effect estimate and the mixed-effects sensitivity analysis. The primary family contains four confirmatory tests and is Holm-adjusted at a family-wise $\alpha=0.05$. Accuracy non-inferiority uses a 5-percentage-point margin at $n\approx300$--400; the planning minimum detectable difference at $n=400$ and a discordant-pair proportion of 0.20 is about 6.3 percentage points unadjusted and 7.5 points after Holm. A dataset with fewer than 300 realised eligible items is reported as underpowered and is not used for a positive claim. HLE is reported separately and is not part of the primary family.

\paragraph*{Threats to validity.}
The first threat is verifier quality. The default verifier is lexical, and the synthetic audit scores 0.58 balanced accuracy. A trust signal built on a weak verifier can amplify a confident wrong majority. The human audit of 150--200 pairs is the gate that must pass before the matrix can support a high-$q$ claim. The second threat is retrieval coverage. The routing and deduplication logic are tested, but the live APIs are not; rate limits, malformed responses, and sparse domains can turn claims into unverifiable ones. The third threat is shared error. Three heterogeneous models reduce but do not eliminate correlated mistakes, and the shared-error floor is a boundary on what any independent verifier can certify.

The fourth threat is benchmark gold. EG-SSS is team-built, so its gold evidence and verdicts require annotation discipline and a defect-rate report. The fifth threat is model-family specificity: the result will be conditional on the three chosen checkpoints and the 96\,GB serving stack. The sixth is stochasticity: the local models run at temperature 0.7, and the optional B7 ceiling uses three samples per question. The analysis averages over seeds where the design allows it and reports the variation that remains.

The main risks are verifier quality, retrieval coverage, annotation throughput, GPU availability, and schedule slip. The verifier and retrieval risks are handled by the N4 audit and the fallback paths. Annotation throughput is handled by the 24-item calibration and the 180/300 ladder. GPU availability is handled by batching the smoke, pilot, and Core runs and by persisting the model cache. Schedule slip is handled by the explicit gates: a failed gate triggers a fix week rather than a silent compression of the matrix.

\section{Summary}\label{sec:design-summary}

Chapter~\ref{chapter:design} fixed the requirements, the system boundary, and the data flow. It defined the debate loop, the claim-level evidence pipeline, the bounded trust operator, the weighted aggregation rule, the injection protocol, and the record contract. It then fixed the experimental design: four executable Core arms, a six-arm Full target, two primary datasets with an HLE ceiling control and the EG-SSS benchmark, final-answer accuracy as the primary endpoint with CMR as the co-primary endpoint, a paired two-seed analysis, and a pre-registered stopping rule. The project plan covers the full project from November 2026 to December 2027, with the final-stack smoke, WP-18, calibration, and verifier audit before the pilot, the Core run, and the Full run. Chapter~\ref{chapter:implementation} describes the implementation and the evidence available at the time of writing.
