# Project Overview & Defense Guide

## What is the Project?
Our project is a teamwork system for computer intelligence models. When asked difficult science questions, multiple computer models discuss the problem together. Instead of letting them blindly agree with a popular wrong answer, our system checks their statements against real scientific research papers and adjusts how much we trust each model based on proven facts.

## Why is this Project Needed?
Computer models can sound very confident even when they are completely wrong. When multiple models talk to each other, they often copy each other's mistakes—just like people following a crowd. If the loudest or first model makes an error, the others tend to follow it out of social pressure, drowning out any model that actually found the right answer. We need a way to stop this false agreement and base decisions on real evidence.

## How Does It Work?
1. **The Discussion:** A group of different computer models discuss a science question over a few rounds.
2. **Checking the Facts:** As they talk, their claims are checked against real scientific libraries and research documents.
3. **Trust Adjustment:** If a model speaks the truth backed by evidence, our system increases our trust in it. If a model spreads false information or just follows the crowd blindly, its influence is reduced.
4. **Final Conclusion:** Instead of a simple popularity vote where every voice counts equally, the final answer is decided by weighting the models based on who is actually supported by real facts.

## Key Defense Questions & Answers

### Why do we need a group discussion instead of just using a single powerful model?
A single model has blind spots and can be confidently wrong without realizing it. When you have multiple models discussing a topic, they can challenge each other, point out flaws in each other's reasoning, and correct mistakes that a single model would miss on its own.

### Why is simple majority voting problematic in scientific reasoning?
Scientific truth is based on facts and evidence, not on a popularity contest. If a majority of models agree on an incorrect answer early on, it creates a peer-pressure effect. The other models abandon their correct answers just to fit in with the group. Relying on a simple vote means the loudest or earliest mistake wins, hiding the real truth.
